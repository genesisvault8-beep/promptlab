Writing AI Marketing Copy That Doesn't Sound Like AI

Specificity beats prompting for "punchy" or "engaging"

There's a reason so much AI-generated marketing copy feels the same: "unlock," "elevate," "seamless," "game-changing," three-item lists with an em dash. That's not a flaw in the model — it's what happens when a prompt is vague enough that the model falls back on the most statistically common way to sound like marketing copy. Here's how to prompt around it.

Ban the stock phrases directly

The single highest-leverage fix: explicitly list the words and patterns you don't want.

Do not use: "unlock," "elevate," "seamless," "game-changing," "revolutionize," "in today's world," or any sentence structure that ends in a rhetorical question. No em-dash lists of three.

Give it a real voice sample

Instead of describing your brand voice abstractly ("friendly but professional"), paste 2-3 sentences you've actually written and ask the model to match that specific register. Models are much better at pattern-matching a concrete example than interpreting an adjective.

Match this voice: "We built this because the existing tools were slow and confusing. This one isn't. That's it — no bigger pitch than that." Now write a 2-sentence product description for [product] in the same voice.

Specify what the copy is competing against

"Write ad copy for my app" gives the model no sense of the actual decision the reader is making. "Write copy for a landing page where the visitor is comparing us against doing nothing (not against a competitor)" changes what argument the copy makes.

Ask for options that differ in strategy, not just wording

Asking for "5 variations" often produces 5 versions of the same idea reworded. Instead, ask for variations that take different angles:

Write 3 versions of this headline: one that leads with a specific number/result, one that leads with the pain point it solves, and one that leads with what makes it different from alternatives.

Cut ruthlessly, or ask the model to

AI copy tends to over-explain. A useful closing instruction: "Now cut this by 40% without losing the core claim." Models are often better at editing down their own draft than at writing tight on the first pass.

Start with a facts brief

The biggest reason AI copy sounds generic is that the model has no real material to work with, so it fills the gap with filler. Give it a short brief of true facts before you ask for any copy:

Product: a budgeting app for freelancers with irregular income. Audience: freelance designers and writers who get paid at unpredictable times. Real proof: 4,000 active users. Average user sets up their first budget in under 5 minutes. Main objection: "I've tried budgeting apps and quit after a week." Use only the facts above. Do not invent numbers, quotes or features.

A before-and-after example

Weak prompt:

Write a catchy tagline for my budgeting app.

Strong prompt:

Using the brief above, write 3 landing page headlines for a visitor who has quit budgeting apps before. One should address the quitting problem directly, one should lead with the 5-minute setup, and one should speak to irregular income. Under 9 words each. Do not use "effortless," "seamless" or "take control."

The second prompt gives the model an argument to make, a reader to picture and a boundary on length and wording. That is what produces copy that reads like it was written for someone.

Use real proof, not adjectives

Vague claims like "powerful" and "easy to use" sound like every other product. Concrete details such as numbers, specific outcomes and real customer language are what make copy believable. Ask the model to convert adjectives into evidence: "For every claim, either attach a fact from the brief or remove the claim."

Match the channel

A headline, an email subject line and a social post follow different rules. Tell the model where the copy will live and what limits apply:

  • Email subject lines: short, specific and honest about what is inside. Ask for a handful of angles.
  • Landing page hero: one clear promise, one supporting line, one action.
  • Ad copy: check the platform's character limits and write to them.
  • Social posts: match the tone people actually use on that platform, and avoid hashtags stuffed in by default.

The edit pass that makes it yours

Even good drafts benefit from a human edit. Read the copy out loud. Wherever you stumble, or wherever it sounds like something you would never say, rewrite it. Swap abstract words for concrete ones, cut any sentence that doesn't add information, and check that every claim is true. Our guide on writing prompts that actually work explains the iteration loop behind this: change one thing at a time and compare.

Common mistakes

  • Asking for "engaging" or "punchy" copy. Those words mean nothing without examples.
  • Publishing the first draft. Treat the first output as raw material.
  • Letting the model invent proof. Always restrict it to facts you supplied.
  • Skipping the audience. Copy for a beginner and copy for an expert should differ.

Frequently asked questions

Can people tell when copy was written by AI?

Often, when it is unedited, because it leans on the same stock phrases and rhythms. Copy that is specific, uses real details and has been edited by a person is much harder to distinguish from human work, and it is also simply better copy.

How many variations should I ask for?

Three to five that take genuinely different angles is usually enough. Ten rewordings of the same idea give you less to choose from than three different strategies.

Can the model write in my brand voice?

It can get close if you give it real examples. Paste two or three sentences you wrote and ask it to match them, and add a short list of words and patterns to avoid. Review the result, since voice is the first thing to drift.

Should I let the model invent statistics or testimonials?

No. Instruct it to use only the facts you provide. Invented numbers and quotes can mislead readers and cause legal and trust problems, so supply real proof and tell the model not to add any.
